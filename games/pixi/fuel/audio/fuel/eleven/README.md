Scene sound effects generated with ElevenLabs Sound Effects v2 (`eleven_text_to_sound_v2`)
on 2026-09-18, flow "Fuel Run — scene SFX": https://elevenlabs.io/app/flows/wFhZCcQuY7BHK5Da6hQw

They replace the Market Stack placeholders (money bag, coins) that Fuel Run inherited when it was
copied. Three takes were generated per event; the ones too short to be usable are kept but disabled
in `assets/audio/sounds.json`, so they can be auditioned and switched on in Crash Composer →
Sound Studio without regenerating.

Post-processing: MP3 → 44.1 kHz mono 16-bit WAV (afconvert), silence trimmed at −40 dB, 6 ms fades,
peak normalized to −1 dBFS, length capped per event; per-event `volume_db` set from measured RMS.

`ride_0.wav` is built differently: no edge fades, and the loop is stitched from the two fastest
takes of the second round (`ride2_b` + `ride2_c`), equal-power crossfaded into each other and then
wrapped back onto the head — 0.88 s, seam under 1 % of peak. Two clips give the loop a longer period
than either alone, so the repeat is harder to hear. Edge fades would pulse audibly on every wrap.

Both dog takes are cut after the first few barks. The raw ones bark twelve to fifteen times in
two seconds, which is a guard dog rather than a dog noticing a bike go by; the cut is made at a
detected onset so it never lands mid-bark.

The loop is rolled off above 3 kHz: the steady take carried 18 % of its energy above 8 kHz and
read as ringing rather than as an engine. Centroid 6.2 kHz down to 4.5 kHz.

The loop does not start with the round: `ride()` in `src/main.ts` holds it back by the
length of the start take minus 300 ms, so the engine is heard catching and the drone comes in
under its tail. Fired together, the start was inaudible. The lead-in reads the clip rather than
hard-coding a delay, so replacing the start take keeps the two in step.

The low end was cut 4 dB below 180 Hz after the steady take turned out to boom: 42 % of its
energy sat under 200 Hz. That is EQ, not level-over-time, so the steadiness is untouched.

The ride was rebuilt from scratch a third time, and the metric that finally explained it was
steadiness, not tone. Level measured over 50 ms windows: the blended loop wandered 2.4 dB with a
9.2 dB range, so it swelled and faded and read as a bike that keeps changing speed. The take in
use wanders 0.5 dB. It was generated by asking for a constant-throttle onboard recording rather
than for a tone, and the loop is cut from its steadiest two seconds, found by sliding a window
and keeping the least variance. Processing is the wrap crossfade and nothing else.

Superseded, kept for the reasoning: the ride is a blend, not a single take, and the reason is worth keeping. Neither generated clip
is an engine on its own: the cruising one is 99 % below 200 Hz — a purr — and the raspy one is
69 % body plus 31 % rasp with no low end at all, which grates. Mixed 55/45 they land at roughly
54 % rumble, 32 % body, 14 % rasp, which is what a bike at speed actually measures like. Soft
saturation was tried first and does not work: harmonics of a 50 Hz rumble are still low.

The ride was rebuilt a second time. The cruising take carried 98.7 % of its energy below 200 Hz
and nothing at all in the 0.8–3 kHz exhaust band, which is why it read as a cat purring rather
than an engine. The take in use now has 31 % of its energy in that band and almost none below
200 Hz. Measuring those bands is the quickest way to tell an engine from a rumble.

The first round of takes idled: firing rate measured 11.9 Hz (~1400 rpm), which reads as standing
still. The replacements run at 22.7 Hz (~2700 rpm). Pitching the old clip up was not an option — it
would have needed ×2.9 and turned the bike into a mosquito.

`start_0.wav` is spliced from two takes: the first 0.30 s of the snappy one, which fires in a
millisecond and then dies away by 0.4 s, crossfaded into the middle of the longer rev, which is
steady enough to carry a tail. Levels are matched across the join or it steps. 1.17 s in total —
long enough to hear the engine settle, still immediate at the front. The other takes stay as
disabled slots.

Take order for `start` is deliberate: slot 0 is the snappy one (0.70 s, energy inside the first
200 ms) because the round has to begin immediately. Slots 1 and 2 are the longer rev and the
multi-second crank, kept disabled for auditioning. A third round generated an explicitly "instant"
start, but that take came back as near-silence at −60 dBFS and was discarded.

Levels are set from the loudest 100 ms window rather than whole-clip RMS: a 0.7 s transient and a
looping drone cannot be compared by average level.

Coins were dropped once the floating popup started counting in multiplier rather than money:
a coin sound promises cash, and cash is no longer what the number says. The tick is now a
counter advancing one notch, which is the sound of a number going up and fits a fuel pump. The
takes all run the whole ratchet, a dozen clicks or more, so a single notch is cut out at its
strongest onset — 0.13 s, one click.

Only takes that actually play are kept. The disabled alternates were 749 KB of audio that

Kept for the record, since the same traps recur: two rounds of coin takes were discarded before that one. Spectra tell the story better than
adjectives: the first said "chime" and the model drew a bell, 5.9 kHz with a 340 ms ring; the
second was glassy at 9.2 kHz, and filtering it down to 5.5 kHz only made it sound synthetic.
The take in use is coins handled in a hand, kept at its own 8.8 kHz with no spectral surgery at
all — metal is bright, and flattening that is what read as a machine.

An earlier note, kept because the reasoning still applies elsewhere: the take in use is a handful of coins rather than one: a single clink read as too sparse for a
payment. It is cut to 0.30 s, which keeps eight distinct clinks and drops the rest of the
scatter. Raw, it was glassy at 9.2 kHz — brighter than the bell that started this — so the
spectrum is rolled off above 1.5 kHz at 1.2 dB per octave, landing at 5.5 kHz: darker than the
bell, close to the single coin, with the cluster intact. Filtering was the only way there; the
prompt asked for "dull" three times and the model kept returning glass.

The first coin prompt said "chime" and the model delivered a bell: 5.9 kHz centroid with a
340 ms ring, which is what made the step tick sound like a chime rather than money. The take in
use now is a physical coin landing — 4.4 kHz, 162 ms — and is cut to 0.24 s, which removes most
of the ring rather than relying on the prompt to avoid it. Its level is 9 dB higher than the
chime's because a clink that short carries far less energy for the same loudness.

The whole progression carries exactly two sounds: the coin tick on every step, and the milestone
on a whole number. `big_sale` used to add a third — a coin cascade — and a run then had three
different money sounds to tell apart. It now plays the same tick as an ordinary step; the
cascade take stays on disk, disabled, if a distinct big-payout cue is ever wanted again.

`sale` deliberately runs one take with no pitch spread: three rotating variants made a run of
steps hard to follow, since every tick sounded like a different event. The other two takes are
kept disabled for auditioning. The single repeated tick is what makes the milestone chime read as
something separate when the multiplier passes 2×, 3× and so on.

The payout ticks were the last Kenney placeholders, borrowed from Explosive Fruits, and read as
vague against the multiplier stepping up. Takes were picked by measurement rather than by ear:
the one leading each event has the fastest attack and the largest share of its energy inside the
first 150 ms, which is what makes a tick read as one event rather than a smear. They are trimmed
to 0.35 s so consecutive steps never overlap. One `big_sale` take came back with a 1.3 s attack —
a slow swell, not a cascade — and was dropped.
